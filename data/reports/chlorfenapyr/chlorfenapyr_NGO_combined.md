**2026-02-09 — French NGOs take BASF to court over banned pesticides, including chlorfenapyr, stored at its Genay plant, and ask that the export/production ban cover biocides with the same active substance, citing a "toxic trade" double standard**
`cross-regime` · `national:FR` · `direct` · `litigation`

- Summary: Générations Futures and Alerte Planète, supported by Extinction Rebellion, filed complaints at the tribunal judiciaire de Lyon. They want an investigation into the holding and marketing of pesticide products made from EU-banned active substances at BASF Agri-Production in Genay. According to the NGOs, an inspection by the regional environment authority (DREAL) found alpha-cypermethrin (Fastac) and also "formulations à base de chlorfénapyr, une autre substance interdite". Générations Futures notes that chlorfenapyr has been out of the EU authorisation process since 2001 but "y est toujours produite". Its demands include a ban on producing, storing, importing and exporting banned pesticides, and "L'extension de cette interdiction aux substances et produits biocides pour lesquelles la substance active est identique à celle d'un pesticide interdit". It also wants France to push this position at EU level.
- Why it matters for the BPR approval: This is the only NGO item in the window that explicitly treats biocide status as a way to keep banned pesticide actives in production. Chlorfenapyr is the clearest example, since it is a non-approved PPP active now under BPR evaluation. The case is national and targets export/production law, not the BPR approval decision. Still, the demand casts the biocide route as a loophole, which could shape how NGOs frame a BPC opinion or Commission decision on chlorfenapyr. The grounds are partly hazard-based: the substance is "banned" because of environmental data gaps and concerns, which carries over strongly. They are also partly trade/policy-based. Effect on the BPR approval is direct in framing but weak in legal reach.
- NGO: Générations Futures (with Alerte Planète, Extinction Rebellion), [Basf fastac pesticides](https://www.generations-futures.fr/actualites/basf-fastac-pesticides/)

`food residues` · `EU` · `indirect–use` · `study/report`

- Summary: foodwatch commissioned lab tests on 64 tea, rice and spice products bought in Austria, France, Germany and the Netherlands in March 2026. It lists chlorfenapyr as the most frequently detected non-approved pesticide ("over 20 detections"). Chlorfenapyr exceeded the 0.01 mg/kg MRL in 3 products: Verstegen paprika powder (NL, 0.103 mg/kg), Ducros paprika doux (FR, 0.096 mg/kg) and REWE Chili mix (DE, 0.061 mg/kg). The report's ECHA export table shows chlorfenapyr as "Not approved / b" (banned for agriculture under the PIC Regulation), exported from 6 EU Member States to 15 third countries in 2024–2025. foodwatch calls for an EU trade ban on non-approved pesticides, automatic lowering of their MRLs to the limit of detection, and rejection of the food and feed safety Omnibus. foodwatch Germany demanded a recall of the REWE product.
- Why it matters for the BPR approval: This is indirect and rests mainly on use-specific exposure: residues in imported food from agricultural use outside the EU. That carries over weakly to PT18 indoor and professional insecticide uses. It does raise chlorfenapyr's public profile as a "banned" substance and connects it to EU export flows. That makes it easier for NGOs to challenge a biocide approval as inconsistent with its PPP status. The report does not mention biocides.
